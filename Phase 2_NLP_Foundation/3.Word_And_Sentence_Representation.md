# Phase 2 — Natural Language Processing (NLP) Foundations
## Topic: Word & Sentence Representations

Computers cannot directly understand the meaning of human language. NLP converts words and sentences into numerical representations so that computers can process them and learn patterns.

For example, a computer needs a numerical representation of "I love AI" before an ML model can work with it.

---

## 1. One-Hot Representations

### Definition
One-hot encoding represents each word as a vector containing only zeros and one 1. Each word has its own unique position.

### Example

Suppose our vocabulary is:

["Cat", "Dog", "Apple"]

Their one-hot representations are:

- Cat → [1, 0, 0]
- Dog → [0, 1, 0]
- Apple → [0, 0, 1]

Each position represents one word in the vocabulary.

### Limitation
One-hot representations do not understand meaning.

For example, "Cat" and "Dog" are more similar in meaning than "Cat" and "Apple", but one-hot encoding treats all three words as equally different.

---

## 2. Word Embeddings

### Definition
Word embeddings represent words as dense numerical vectors that can capture relationships in meaning and usage.

Unlike one-hot vectors, which contain mostly zeros, embeddings usually contain real numbers.

### Example

Illustrative vectors:

- Cat → [0.8, 0.6, 0.2]
- Dog → [0.7, 0.7, 0.3]
- Car → [-0.5, 0.1, 0.9]

The vectors for "Cat" and "Dog" might be closer together because they have related meanings and uses.

These numbers are only examples, not actual trained embeddings.

### Why Are They Useful?
- Help models learn relationships between words.
- Represent words using fewer dimensions than large one-hot vectors.
- Support tasks such as text classification and semantic similarity.

---

## 3. Word2Vec — Concept

### Definition
Word2Vec is a technique that learns word embeddings by examining how words appear in surrounding contexts.

The main idea is that words appearing in similar contexts often have related meanings.

### Example

Consider these sentences:

- "The cat drinks milk."
- "The dog drinks milk."
- "The cat plays outside."
- "The dog plays outside."

Because "cat" and "dog" appear in similar contexts, Word2Vec may learn similar vectors for them.

### Two Main Approaches

**1. CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words)**

Predicts a target word from its surrounding words.

Example:

"The cat drinks ___."

The model learns to predict a suitable missing word, such as "milk", from context.

**2. Skip-gram**

Predicts surrounding words from a target word.

Example:

Given "cat", the model learns to predict words that commonly occur nearby, such as "the", "drinks", or "plays".

Important: Word2Vec learns one main vector per word, so the word "bank" generally has the same embedding whether it refers to a riverbank or a financial institution.

---

## 4. Contextual Representations

### Definition
Contextual representations generate word representations based on the surrounding words in a sentence.

The representation of a word can change depending on its meaning in context.

### Example

Sentence 1:
"I deposited money at the bank."

Here, "bank" refers to a financial institution.

Sentence 2:
"We sat beside the river bank."

Here, "bank" refers to land beside a river.

A contextual model can generate different representations of "bank" because the surrounding words indicate different meanings.

### Examples of Models
- BERT
- GPT

These models use context to build representations of words or tokens.

---

## 5. Static vs. Contextual Embeddings

### Static Embeddings
Each word has the same learned vector regardless of the sentence in which it appears.

Examples:
- Word2Vec
- GloVe

### Contextual Embeddings
A word's representation depends on its surrounding words.

Examples:
- BERT
- GPT

### Comparison

| Feature | Static Embeddings | Contextual Embeddings |
|---|---|---|
| Representation | One main vector per word | Changes with context |
| Word meaning | Does not directly distinguish different meanings of the same word | Can represent different meanings |
| Example models | Word2Vec, GloVe | BERT, GPT |
| Complexity | Generally simpler | Generally more computationally expensive |

Example:

The word "bat":

- "The bat flew at night."
- "He hit the ball with a bat."

Static embeddings use the same main word vector for "bat".

Contextual representations can distinguish the flying animal from the sports equipment.

---

## 6. Why Are Semantic Representations Needed?

### Definition
Semantic representations help computers capture the meaning and relationships of words or sentences instead of relying only on exact word matches.

### Example

Sentence A:
"I am happy."

Sentence B:
"I feel joyful."

The words are different, but their meanings are similar.

A basic Bag of Words model may struggle to recognize this relationship because the sentences share no words.

Word embeddings and contextual representations can help identify that the sentences have similar meanings.

### Real-World Applications

- **Search engines:** Find relevant results even when a user uses different words.
- **Chatbots:** Match a user's question with a similar question in a knowledge base.
- **Recruitment systems:** Compare a candidate's skills with job requirements using related terms.
- **Sentiment analysis:** Help models interpret words and phrases in context.
- **Text similarity:** Identify sentences or documents with related meanings.

### Key Takeaway

One-hot encoding identifies words using separate positions, word embeddings learn numerical representations of word relationships, Word2Vec learns embeddings from surrounding words, and contextual models adjust representations according to sentence meaning.

Semantic representations are important because **understanding relationships in meaning is more useful than simply counting words.**
